  Space-themed STEM content often oversimplifies the engineering trade-offs
  that define a real mission or presents them at a level too complex to hold a
  young learner's attention. Few tools make those trade-offs both tangible and
  fun. Your challenge is to design and build an interactive game or app that
  lets students run a lunar or Martian outpost, balancing competing demands
  like life support, radiation shielding, power, and food production, so they
  experience firsthand the decisions that determine whether a mission fails or
  succeeds.


  Background
  As NASA works to establish a sustained presence on the Moon and extend human
  exploration toward Mars, one of the most persistent challenges isn't just
  engineering hardware — it's building public understanding of what survival
  actually requires in these environments. Astronauts on a lunar or Martian
  outpost must balance competing demands: limited power has to be split between
  life support, communications, and science; radiation exposure has to be
  weighed against mission objectives; and every decision carries a resource
  cost that compounds over time. These are the same kinds of trade-offs NASA's
  own mission planners navigate daily, informed by real data — solar activity
  and radiation risk from the Space Weather Database (DONKI), astronaut
  radiation dosimetry and biological research from the Open Science Data
  Repository (OSDR), and environmental data from Artemis and Mars surface
  missions.

  The gap is in translation. Space education content for kids often stays too
  abstract (e.g., posters and general facts) to convey real trade-offs or
  assumes a technical background too advanced for a young audience to engage
  with meaningfully. As a result, many students never encounter the actual
  decision-making logic behind a mission—only its outcomes. Content that leans
  purely educational often struggles to hold attention; content that leans
  purely toward entertainment can lose accuracy.

  Participants might prioritize designing mechanics—not just visuals—that
  mirror real constraints: a choice that saves power today might cost oxygen
  tomorrow. Grounding gameplay in real NASA open data, rather than invented
  statistics, is encouraged. Possible approaches include survival-style
  resource management, narrative decision trees, or dashboard-style mission
  control simulators—but participants are encouraged to explore whatever format
  best teaches the underlying trade-off rather than conforming to any single
  template.

  Objectives
  Your challenge is to design and build an interactive game or app that lets
  kids ages 7–12 manage a lunar or Martian outpost, making real decisions about
  life support, radiation shielding, power budgeting, food production, and
  communication delay, and experiencing how those choices play out over the
  course of a simulated mission.

  Think about how your tool can turn genuine mission constraints into gameplay:
  what does it feel like to trade oxygen reserves for an extra research
  opportunity, or to reinforce shielding against a radiation event at the cost
  of tonight's power supply? How will you represent competing resources so that
  no choice is free, and every decision teaches something true about what
  astronauts and mission planners actually navigate?

  You're encouraged to ground your simulation in real NASA open data wherever
  possible — for example, using DONKI to reflect actual solar activity and
  radiation risk, the OSDR for real astronaut health and biological research
  findings, or Artemis and Mars surface mission data to inform environmental
  conditions at your chosen site. Incorporating live or archived NASA datasets,
  rather than fabricated numbers, will make the experience more authentic and
  give players a genuine window into how mission-critical decisions are made.

  Format is open: you could build a resource-management survival game, a
  narrative-driven decision simulator, a mission-control-style dashboard
  experience, or something else entirely — browser-based, mobile, or desktop.
  The goal is a working prototype that demonstrates the trade-off mechanic
  clearly enough that a young player walks away understanding a real constraint
  of deep space survival, not just having played a fun game. Creative
  interpretations of "outpost," "site," or "mission" beyond the Moon and Mars
  are welcome, provided the underlying educational goal — teaching real
  engineering trade-offs through play — stays central.

  Potential Considerations
  You may (but are not required to) consider the following:

  Audience and Accessibility

  Your target audience is kids ages 7–12, but consider whether your tool can
  flex to engage older siblings, parents, or classroom settings too.
  Favor plain language over jargon; where a scientific term is unavoidable —
  radiation dose, microgravity, sol — briefly explain it in-context rather than
  assuming prior knowledge.
  Low barrier to entry matters — a browser-playable experience with no download
  or account required will reach more participants during judging and more
  students afterward.
  Grounding in Real Data

  Wherever possible, pull from NASA's public APIs and open datasets rather than
  inventing plausible-sounding numbers—even a small amount of real data—a real
  radiation reading, a real Mars photo, a real astronaut health finding—makes
  the experience meaningfully more credible than an entirely simulated one.
  Clearly attribute your data sources within the tool itself, so players
  understand they're engaging with real NASA science, not just a themed game.
  If live API calls aren't feasible during your build window, a curated real
  dataset embedded directly into your tool is a reasonable and honest
  alternative—just don't present static data as live.
  Gameplay and Design Pitfalls to Avoid

  Avoid designing a "safe" choice that has no cost—the educational value comes
  from every decision involving a genuine trade-off, mirroring real mission
  planning.
  Don't let visual polish substitute for mechanical accuracy—a simple game that
  teaches a real constraint beats a beautiful one that doesn't.
  Avoid overwhelming young players with too many simultaneous systems to track;
  two or three core resources—life support, power, shielding—are usually more
  effective than five or six.
  What Would Make a Solution Stand Out

  Tools that let players see the real-world consequences of their in-game
  choice—linking a radiation-shielding decision to actual current space weather
  data.
  Replayability—mechanics or events that vary between playthroughs encourage
  kids to try different strategies and learn from failure without it feeling
  punitive.
  Accompanying a working prototype with a short explanation of which NASA
  datasets were used and why, making the science traceable and verifiable by
  judges.

  at the top of the page. More resources may be added before the hackathon
  begins.

  ## 1. Design rules

  1. **Every choice costs something.** Each option either loses a resource or
  misses science. The challenge brief specifically warns against "safe"
  choices.
  2. **Today's choice can cost tomorrow.** Three choices set a *memory flag*
  that changes a later day. This is the challenge's "save power today, cost
  oxygen tomorrow" idea.
  3. **One situation per day, 14 decisions in total.** This keeps it simple for
  7-year-olds.
  4. **Plain language.** Each situation is at most ~40 words and each option at
  most ~15. Every science word is explained the moment it appears.
  5. **Every choice ends with a real fact.** After choosing, the player sees a
  short **"Real Space Fact"** card with a NASA source. This is how the game
  *teaches*.
  6. **Replayability.** Day 6 uses a randomly picked **real solar flare from
  NASA DONKI**, and Day 11 picks 1 of 2 random events. Each playthrough is a
  little different.
  7. **No new resources.** "Food production" from the brief is covered by the
  greenhouse story (plants make oxygen), so there is no 4th bar.

  ### How choices are shown to the player
  Decision cards show **arrows, not numbers**: `O₂ ▼  ⚡ ▲  ★ ▲`. Kids then
  reason about trade-offs ("this saves power but costs air") instead of doing
  maths. The exact numbers below stay in the game's data files.

  ### Key
  | Symbol | Meaning |
  |---|---|
  | **O₂** | Life Support (oxygen) |
  | **⚡** | Power (battery) |
  | **☢** | Radiation exposure. It goes *up* when bad. Shown as Shield in the
  UI if you choose that option (open question Q3) |
  | **★** | Science points |
  | 🏳 | Sets a memory flag used on a later day |
  | 🗺 | Map objects to highlight |

  > ⚠️ **All numbers are placeholder game-balancing values, not scientific
  measurements.** They get tuned in Phase 8 with the balancing script.
